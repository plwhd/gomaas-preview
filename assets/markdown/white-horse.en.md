# The White Horse Discourse: Names, Classification, and Judgment

The first difficulty with “a white horse is not a horse” is deciding what, exactly, the statement denies.

It might mean that white horses do not belong to the category of horses. It might mean that “white horse” and “horse” differ in meaning. Or it might ask what we retain, and what we leave out, when we call a white horse a horse.

These questions are related, but they cannot all be answered in the same way. Ending the discussion with “of course a white horse is a horse” can overlook the text’s close attention to names. Interpreting “not” as “different from” still leaves us to explain why the dialogue becomes a dispute.

Starting from several key passages, this article compares two main readings: one treats the dialogue as an argument about classification; the other treats it as an analysis of names and the meanings formed by combining them. The question is **which passages each reading can explain, and what difficulties it must address.** A hypothetical example of a language model summarizing research then considers how this way of reading can be useful today.

## Original Passages and Paraphrases

The passages below are selected for their bearing on the analysis that follows. Terms in the classical text such as *fei* (“not”), *wei* (“being” or “counting as”), and *li* (“separating”) allow room for interpretation. The paraphrases are therefore a starting point for reading, rather than a substitute for assessing the arguments.

### Naming Form and Naming Color

> “白马非马，可乎？”曰：“可。”曰：“何哉？”曰：“马者，所以命形也；白者，所以命色也；命色者，非命形也。故曰白马非马。”

“Can one say that a white horse is not a horse?” “Yes.” “Why?” “‘Horse’ names a form; ‘white’ names a color. Naming a color differs from naming a form. Hence, a white horse is not a horse.”

The passage first distinguishes the roles of “horse” and “white.” It has not yet fully explained why combining the two warrants the conclusion that “a white horse is not a horse.”

### Having a White Horse and Asking for a White Horse

> “有白马，不可谓无马也。不可谓无马者，非马也？有白马为有马，白之非马，何也？”

The objector says: if there is a white horse, one cannot say there is no horse. Since having a white horse counts as having a horse, why say that a white horse is not a horse?

The reply is:

> “求马，黄、黑马皆可致；求白马，黄、黑马不可致。使白马乃马也，是所求一也。”

If someone asks for a horse, either a yellow horse or a black horse may be brought. If someone asks for a white horse, neither satisfies the request. If “white horse” and “horse” were the same, the two requests should also be the same.

The two sides have begun to focus on different questions. The objector asks what is present; the respondent turns to what is requested.

### Setting Whiteness Aside

> “有白马不可谓无马者，离白之谓也。不离者，有白马不可谓有马也。”

On one interpretation, moving from “there is a white horse” to “there is a horse” requires separating out, or setting aside, the qualification “white.” If that qualification is retained in full, the two statements cannot be treated as the same.

Researchers disagree both on how to understand “separating” here and on which side of the dialogue speaks this passage. The discussion below examines the problem it raises, without relying on a definite attribution of the speaker to settle the meaning of the whole text.

### Specifying Whiteness and Selecting by Color

> “白者不定所白，忘之而可也。白马者言白，定所白也。定所白者，非白也。”

One relatively clear distinction is that “white,” used on its own, does not yet specify what is white. In “white horse,” it does specify the object it describes. Whiteness qualified in this way differs from whiteness without that qualification.

The text then says:

> “马者，无去取于色，故黄、黑马皆所以应；白马者，有去取于色，黄、黑马皆以所色去，故唯白马独可以应耳。无去者，非有去也。故曰白马非马。”

The request “horse” does not select by color, so yellow and black horses can both satisfy it. “White horse” does select by color: yellow and black horses are excluded, and only a white horse meets the request. Not selecting by color differs from selecting by color.

These quotations come from [the “White Horse Discourse” in the Gongsun Longzi](https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hans/公孫龍子/2). The webpage includes later commentary; only the main text is quoted here.

## Why the Argument from “Asking for a Horse” Is Persuasive

Suppose someone wants to buy a horse. A seller brings a black horse, and the request can be met. If the buyer specifically wants a white horse, that same black horse cannot be delivered in fulfillment of the request.

The example turns a difference between names into a practical condition of selection. Color is not an optional flourish: it determines which objects are acceptable.

But what does this prove?

If the conclusion is that “white horse” and “horse” cannot be substituted for each other in every context, the argument is strong. If the conclusion is that white horses do not belong to the category of horses, another step is needed.

The objector’s claim that “having a white horse counts as having a horse” can be understood as a one-way relation: wherever there is a white horse, there is a horse. The converse need not hold. Having a horse does not necessarily mean having a white horse; asking for a horse does not necessarily mean asking for a white horse.

The following can therefore both be true:

- A white horse can satisfy the request “horse.”
- A black horse can satisfy the request “horse,” but not the request “white horse.”

The first supports classifying white horses as horses. The second shows that the two requests differ. There is no contradiction between them.

The problem lies in the step “if a white horse were a horse, what is requested would be the same.” It moves from “a white horse is a horse” to “the two requests should be identical.” Under an ordinary understanding of classification, this move is unsupported: a more specific category’s belonging to a more general category does not mean that the two have identical selection conditions.

This is a criticism of the inference. It does not yet establish that Gongsun Long himself merely misunderstood classification, because “not” may not be intended to deny category membership.

## If “Not” Means Only “Not the Same”

Consider a stronger defense of Gongsun Long:

> He does not deny that a white horse is also a horse. He wants to show that what “white horse” expresses should not be confused with what “horse” expresses. Would answering him in terms of category membership miss the point?

This defense deserves serious consideration. The text repeatedly discusses naming form, naming color, what is requested, and what is accepted or excluded. It does attend to how names are used and which conditions are retained. If “not” indicates a difference in meaning or requirements, the argument from asking for a horse no longer appears absurd.

This reading must still explain why the objector remains unconvinced.

If both sides accept that “white horse” and “horse” differ in meaning, and both accept that having a white horse entails having a horse, their positions seem able to coexist. Their dispute may concern what each takes to be an adequate answer.

The objector’s position is that, once a particular white horse is present, a horse is present. Its color does not cancel that fact.

The other side asks whether moving from “white horse” to “horse” has already changed the content of the description. Only “horse” has been retained, yet this more general description is being offered as an answer to a question about “white horse.”

A more substantial disagreement can thus be reconstructed: **Can a more general name correctly describe an object with specific qualifications? Must such a description also acknowledge that it does not retain all of the object’s determinations?**

The first question concerns whether the classification holds; the second concerns whether the description is sufficient. An answer can be true yet insufficient. It can also be sufficient for a particular question without reporting every piece of information.

For example, if someone asks whether there are any horses in the yard, “there is a white horse” is enough. If someone asks whether there are any white horses, “there is a horse” does not settle the matter. There is no single wording that is always the right one: the answer must be assessed together with the question.

Replacing “not” with “not the same” can therefore remove some misunderstandings. It still needs to be made clear whether the difference concerns names, requests, or the categories to which objects belong. Different relations license different inferences; a milder expression alone cannot merge them.

## Does “Separating Whiteness” Analyze the Object or Omit Information?

“Separating whiteness” sharpens the problem.

Imagine a white horse that is three years old and has previously injured its left leg. We could describe it in several ways:

“There is a three-year-old white horse with a past injury to its left leg.”

“There is a white horse.”

“There is a horse.”

These statements progressively omit information, but they can describe the same object. Moving from the first to the last does not require making the horse younger, healing its leg, or removing its white color.

There are at least two different senses of “separating” here:

One sets an attribute aside in thought or description. The other divides the object itself into independent things. The first can occur without the second occurring.

If “separating whiteness” refers to the first process, it reveals the cost of generalization: “there is a horse” does not report everything contained in “there is a white horse.” This is a valid and substantive observation. It draws attention to how often general judgments depend on leaving something out.

It still does not follow that the resulting judgment is incorrect. General names allow us to retain certain shared features without reporting every other difference. Otherwise, a horse’s color, age, and injury history would all have to enter its name, leaving us scarcely able to make a concise judgment about its category.

A defender of “separating whiteness” might reply that the point is not to forbid generalization, but to reject treating the generalized content as the original content in full.

That reply is reasonable. It also clarifies the limits of the dispute:

“There is a white horse” supports “there is a horse,” but “there is a horse” cannot, on its own, recover all the information in “there is a white horse.” Generalization permits an inference; it does not guarantee that no information is lost.

On this reading, “separating whiteness” helps explain the transition between two descriptions. It does not force us to deny that the transition is valid.

## “Specifying Whiteness” Raises a More Detailed Question

If “separating whiteness” concerns moving from a more specific description to a more general one, “specifying whiteness” concerns how a name is composed internally.

“White,” on its own, can describe paper, walls, clothing, or horses. In “white horse,” it has been specified as the horse’s color.

A room containing a sheet of white paper and a black horse does not thereby contain a white horse. Although both whiteness and a horse are present, they do not stand in the relation required by “white horse.”

Understanding “white horse” therefore requires understanding that the whiteness belongs to that horse. Combining words not only adds content; it also specifies how the contents are related.

In “Names, Things, and Compound Nouns in Gongsun Long’s White Horse Discourse,” Liu Yuyu and Ren Yuan give “separating whiteness” and “specifying whiteness” a central role in interpretation. They propose reading the debate through compositional semantics: rather than simply denying the common-sense claim that a white horse is a horse, Gongsun Long is asking under what conditions that description holds and how compound names relate to what they describe. [See the paper](https://www.enaea.edu.cn/sztsg/wenhuakejisuyang/zx/114573.html).

This approach gives a clear role to the passage about specifying what is white. If the whole dialogue is treated merely as a classification puzzle about whether white horses are horses, these lines can easily appear unnecessary.

Explaining their role, however, does not establish a single possible meaning for the entire text. We must still ask whether the reading accounts for the objections in the dialogue, whether it requires supplying principles that the text leaves unstated, and whether all the passages consistently address the same relation.

In his research overview for the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Chris Fraser presents several interpretive approaches and cautions against treating the wish to make an argument reasonable as an unconditional license to supply the text with a theory. Different readings may fit different passages, but they still need to be tested against the text as a whole. [See “School of Names”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/school-names/).

These approaches create a useful tension. One seeks to reconstruct a coherent argument; the other warns that reconstruction can go too far. Assessing an interpretation requires considering both its explanatory power and the assumptions it asks us to add to the text.

The passages selected here support the view that “specifying whiteness” concerns modification and qualification. They do not suffice to show that Gongsun Long had developed a complete, unambiguous theory of semantics.

## Riding a White Horse and Asking for One: When Substitution Works

The “Lesser Illustrations” chapter of the Mozi offers a clear pair of statements:

> “白马，马也；乘白马，乘马也。”

A white horse is a horse; riding a white horse is riding a horse. [See the Mozi, “Lesser Illustrations”](https://ctext.org/mozi/minor-illustrations/zhs).

Placing this beside the example of asking for a white horse helps distinguish the roles that names play in different sentences.

If someone rides a white horse through a city gate, saying that the person “rode a horse through the gate” would ordinarily not misdescribe the action. The sentence does not report the color, but it preserves the action being described.

If someone requests a white horse, bringing a black horse does not fulfill the request. Color determines whether the action succeeds and cannot simply be omitted.

Substitution between “white horse” and “horse” need not therefore be either permitted everywhere or prohibited everywhere. We need to ask whether the original judgment still holds, or the original request is still met, after the substitution.

Hu Shi discusses related distinctions in his “New Explanations of the ‘Lesser Illustrations’ Chapter of the Mozi,” proposing “different from” to clarify the sense of “not.” [See Hu Shi’s text](https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hans/《墨子·小取》篇新詁). Taking this approach further requires specifying the relation in which the difference occurs.

“Riding a white horse” and “riding a horse” can both be true as descriptions of a completed action. “Asking for a white horse” and “asking for a horse” cannot be treated as identical when setting conditions of acceptance. This distinction requires neither denying that a white horse is a horse nor assuming that the names are interchangeable everywhere.

It shifts the dispute from a general question of “is it or isn’t it?” to particular questions about inference: which statement follows from which, and which qualifications remain in force?

## What Can We Conclude About the White Horse Discourse?

We can now assess the two readings separately.

If “a white horse is not a horse” means that white horses do not belong to the category of horses, the difference between the requests does not support the conclusion. The one-way relation maintained by the objector—having a white horse entails having a horse—has not been overturned.

If it means that “white horse” and “horse” differ in meaning and requirements, the argument has considerable force. Asking for a horse shows a difference in selection conditions; separating whiteness concerns information omitted in generalization; specifying whiteness concerns qualification within a compound name.

The latter reading explains more of the semantic detail. It must still explain why the text expresses these differences as “not” while continuing to answer the objection that having a white horse counts as having a horse. We cannot interpret “not” as a difference in meaning and simultaneously treat the objector’s claim about classification as refuted.

This article leans toward reading these passages as a debate about names, qualifications, and the conditions under which descriptions hold. The text provides grounds for this approach, but it does not amount to a final judgment on everything the author intended.

The “Correct Naming” chapter of the Xunzi offers another perspective worth considering alongside it: how names acquire public conventions and establish distinctions between the general and the specific, enabling people to understand things together. [See the Xunzi, “Correct Naming”](https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hans/荀子/正名篇).

This raises a further question: must an accurate name retain as many specific differences as possible, or can accuracy also involve generalizing at an appropriate level?

Both demands arise in language use. An order must retain its decisive qualifications; classification must allow different objects to share a general name. Applying either demand to every context would deprive language of some of its uses.

The “Under Heaven” chapter of the Zhuangzi says of disputers:

> “能胜人之口，不能服人之心。”

They could defeat others in argument, but could not convince them. This remark cannot substitute for an assessment of the White Horse Discourse itself. It does remind us that leaving someone momentarily unable to reply and genuinely resolving a disagreement are not always the same thing. [See the Zhuangzi, “Under Heaven”](https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hans/莊子/天下).

An interpretation’s value depends on whether it explains something previously left unexplained. Prompting us to reconsider common sense is a beginning. Explaining what should be retained or revised afterward is what moves the discussion forward.

## Summarizing Research with Language Models Can Also Drop Qualifications

Similar shifts in scope often arise when language models are used to read and summarize articles. The following is a hypothetical example, not a record of a tested model output.

A paper proposes:

“The White Horse Discourse can be interpreted in terms of compound names.”

A summary turns this into:

“Researchers believe that compound names are the central concern of the White Horse Discourse.”

A further summary produces:

“Scholars have proved that Gongsun Long’s original intention was to discuss compound names.”

More than the wording has changed.

The first sentence offers a possible interpretation. The second obscures which particular authors hold that view. The third adds assertions of consensus, proof, and historical intention. The original qualifications gradually disappear while the conclusion grows stronger.

Even if a real paper remains among the references, it may not support the final sentence. Checking a source means not only establishing that it exists, but also determining how far its evidence reaches.

Research on the White Horse Discourse is especially vulnerable to this kind of summarization. Studies explaining compound names, testing the validity of inferences, and examining the text’s historical context do not automatically lead to the same conclusion. Combining them under “scholars have shown” can make disagreements disappear into a fluent account.

The earlier analysis of separating whiteness is useful here: omitting content does not necessarily introduce an error, but the resulting statement cannot support a stronger judgment that the omitted qualifications would rule out.

Moving from “some researchers propose this interpretation” to “this interpretation exists” is a valid generalization. Moving from the former to “this is what the author intended” adds a claim that requires further evidence.

This does not use an ancient debate to explain the technical workings of language models. It offers a way to check writing: examine how a revision changes a judgment’s scope, paying particular attention to whether possibility, quantity, conditions, and attribution have been retained.

The more complete and natural a model-generated article reads, the more important this check becomes. Smooth transitions between sentences do not guarantee that every inference is warranted.

## The White Horse Is Still There

A white horse can be called a “white horse” or a “horse.” The two names do not report the same amount of information, but they can refer to the same object.

When someone asks whether there is a horse, its color can go unreported for the moment. When someone requests a white horse, color becomes a condition that cannot be omitted. The difference comes from the question being answered and the role the name plays in it.

The White Horse Discourse makes this ordinary use of language worth examining. It shows how generalization leaves information out, qualification changes requirements, and combinations of words specify relations between different contents.

Whether these observations support “a white horse is not a horse” depends on how “not” is understood. Denying category membership and identifying a difference in meaning require different arguments. Readers have reason to accept the difference in meaning, and also to reject using it to deny category membership.

The white horse does not lose its whiteness when it is called a “horse.” What we have left unsaid needs explaining. What further conclusions we may draw needs evidence too.
